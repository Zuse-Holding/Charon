Most people researching companies end up choosing a tool the same way: they hit a paywall on Crunchbase, get quoted a number by PitchBook that makes them close the tab, and start looking for something in between. That gap is real, and it's worth understanding what each tool is actually optimized for before you pick one.

## Crunchbase: a directory you search

Crunchbase's core product is a searchable directory of companies, investors, and funding events. It's strong where its data is self-reported or scraped at scale — funding rounds, basic firmographics, contact info. What it doesn't do is synthesize that data into an answer. You still have to open several profiles, compare them, and write the analysis yourself.

That's fine if your job is finding leads or checking a funding round. It's slower if your job is answering "should I care about this company," because the tool stops at data access.

## PitchBook: deep data, enterprise process

PitchBook's datasets go deeper — private-market financials, deal comps, LP data — but the tradeoff is the buying process. There's no public pricing page, no self-serve signup, and quotes commonly land in five figures a year before add-on modules. It's built for institutional desks that need exhaustive coverage and have a procurement process to match.

If you're an individual analyst, a founder doing competitive research, or a small team, that process is the wrong shape for the problem — you don't need the whole dataset, you need an answer today.

## Where data fusion fits

The alternative approach — what Metis is built around — is data fusion rather than data access: pulling from public sources (news, filings, the company's own site, product data) and synthesizing them into one report automatically, rather than handing you a directory entry to interpret.

The practical difference shows up in time-to-answer. A directory tool gets you data points. A fusion tool gets you a narrative — leadership, funding history, competitive context, and a verdict — without you doing the cross-referencing.

## How to actually choose

A few questions that tend to clarify it fast:

- **Do you need raw private-market datasets at scale, or do you need an answer on a specific entity?** The former points to PitchBook; the latter points to something lighter.
- **Is your budget a monthly self-serve line item, or does it need to go through procurement?** If it's the former, PitchBook's buying process is a mismatch regardless of the data quality.
- **How much of your time goes into synthesis versus lookup?** If you're spending more time connecting dots across sources than finding them, a fusion-first tool will save more time than a bigger directory will.

There's no universally right answer — a large institutional research desk still has good reasons to run PitchBook. But for the individual-to-small-team research workflow that most people are actually doing, the deciding factor is usually speed to a synthesized answer, not the size of the underlying database.
